## Thesis

AI doesn't create new risk. It accelerates whatever governance gap already exists — across the data it touches, the vendors it depends on, and the people it employs.

## Final caption

AI is quietly reshaping three things inside most organisations: the data it touches, the vendors it depends on, and the people it employs. We've watched versions of this pattern repeat across telecom operations centres, hospital back-offices and fintech compliance teams — different job titles, same shape.

Three assumptions are forming faster than the governance behind them:

→ Data — "Our vendor is SOC 2 and ISO 27001 certified, so our governance obligations are covered." Certification protects their infrastructure. It says nothing about what your employees paste into a prompt, or where that data trains a model next.

→ Vendors — "This workflow is core to how the team operates now, and it'll keep working exactly as designed, for as long as we need it to." The roadmap it depends on belongs to a company that owes you nothing beyond the current contract.

→ People — "If a five-person function can now run on two, the responsible move is headcount reduction." Maybe. Or maybe the responsible move is redesigning what the other three should be doing instead — and reduction is what you decide after that redesign, not instead of it.

The starting discipline is simple, even if the execution isn't: name an owner for every AI-touched process, set a boundary on what it's allowed to decide alone, and revisit that boundary on a fixed schedule — not when something breaks.
